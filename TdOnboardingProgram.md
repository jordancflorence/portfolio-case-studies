# Case Study: TD Business Banking: Credit Fulfillment Onboarding Program

**Role:** Operations Officer III, TD Business Banking Credit Fulfillment
**Timeline:** Approximately 2024
**Format:** Self-initiated program design; adopted by team leadership

---

## Context

TD Business Banking's credit fulfillment operations team processes funding for business credit deals under a strict 24-hour SLA. The work ranges from routine credit transactions to complex multi-party deals worth up to $750,000, including business acquisitions, real estate closings, and government-backed funding arrangements. The stakes are high, the pace is relentless, and the compliance requirements are non-negotiable.

When I joined the team as a new Operations Officer, no formal onboarding program existed. New officers learned by shadowing tenured colleagues. Full system access took six to eight weeks to obtain. The procedure library was incomplete and frequently out of sync with actual compliance requirements. Personal note-taking was treated as a mandatory survival skill in place of reliable institutional documentation.

Within my first week, my team manager of over twenty years announced her departure. Her superior left shortly after. The team was left without leadership during one of the most demanding onboarding periods I have experienced in my career.

---

## The Problem

The gap between what a new officer needed to know and what the existing onboarding process provided was significant, and the consequences of that gap were not abstract. Officers working in this environment were responsible for time-sensitive funding decisions in a compliance-driven context with no margin for error.

The existing approach created three compounding problems. Knowledge transfer depended entirely on the availability and consistency of tenured officers, creating bottlenecks and variation in what new hires actually learned. The procedure library did not reflect current compliance requirements, meaning officers either relied on outdated documentation or developed informal workarounds through personal notes. And there was no structured progression from supervised to independent work: no quality checkpoints, no milestones, no way to know whether an officer was genuinely ready to handle complex deals on their own.

I experienced all of this firsthand in my first several months. I decided something should be done about it.

---

## The Approach

This program was not assigned to me. It was not part of my job description. It came entirely from my own experience of what it felt like to onboard into a role that had no infrastructure to support me, and my belief that officers joining after me should not have to experience the same.

I began with requirements gathering. I conducted informal interviews with tenured officers to understand their workflows, the pain points they encountered consistently, and the gaps they had identified between the procedure library and actual practice. I ran pain point sessions with the broader team during the three-month period when leadership was absent, creating a structured space for officers to surface concerns and contribute to what a better onboarding experience might look like.

Because I was still new to the role myself, I deliberately sought out expertise I didn't have. I worked closely with an internal Business Analyst who had previously held my role and had since transitioned into a strategic capacity. He helped me identify the quality metrics that a compliance-driven environment required: the specific criteria that would determine whether an officer was genuinely ready to work independently, not just whether they had been in the role long enough.

The program I built included:

- A structured onboarding schedule that sequenced learning across the first weeks in role
- Knowledge retention checkpoints embedded throughout the schedule
- A graduated progression model: officers moved from supervised to independent work based on demonstrated competency against quality metrics, not on time elapsed
- Knowledge transfer documentation designed to ensure onboarding did not depend on any single person's availability or institutional memory
- Coverage of both standard credit deals and the more complex multi-party transactions that represented the highest-stakes work on the team

The goal was not to cover everything. In a role with as many variables as credit fulfillment operations, comprehensive documentation is neither achievable nor sufficient. The goal was to ensure that new officers were structurally set up to handle the pace, and that the program itself could withstand personnel changes without degrading.

---

## The Outcome

When the incoming team manager arrived three months into the process, the program was reviewed, adopted, and handed off to an internal Business Analyst for ongoing stewardship.

I left TD shortly after the new manager came on board. I do not know the full extent of what was implemented in the final form. What I do know is that feedback I received around the time of my departure indicated that the incoming manager had actioned the core criteria I had proposed, and that the structural gaps I had experienced as a new officer were being addressed for those who came after.

The program was built by someone who had just lived the problem it was designed to solve. That proximity was both the motivation and the methodology.

---

## What This Demonstrates

This project is an example of what I do when I encounter a broken process and have the capacity to address it: I identify the gap, gather requirements from the people closest to the work, collaborate with subject matter experts to fill what I don't know, and build something structured enough to outlast my own involvement.

The skills involved (stakeholder interviews, requirements gathering, quality metric design, documentation, and cross-functional collaboration with business analysts) are the same skills I bring to process improvement and business analyst roles today. The context was a high-stakes regulated banking environment. The output was a program that was adopted and handed off without me having to campaign for it further.

I started it because I believed it was necessary. I finished it because I believed the officers who came after me deserved better than what I had.

---

## Tools and Methods Used

- Stakeholder interviews with tenured officers and team leads
- Pain point facilitation sessions with the broader team
- Collaboration with internal Business Analyst (quality metric design)
- Structured documentation and schedule design
- Graduated competency progression framework
