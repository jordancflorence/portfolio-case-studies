# Case Study: TD Online Banking — Bill Payment Workflow Redesign

**Role:** Personal Banking Associate, TD Canada Trust
**Timeline:** Approximately 2023
**Format:** Internal ideation submission with supporting UI mockup

---

## Context

TD Canada Trust's EasyWeb online banking platform underwent a significant UI refresh. The update modernized the interface but left a gap in one of the most common client workflows: paying multiple bills in a single session.

As the branch's go-to resource for technology questions, I regularly fielded client feedback about their digital banking experience, not through formal channels, but through direct conversation at my desk. Clients trusted me to listen and, where I could, to help.

---

## The Problem

After the UI refresh, clients making multiple bill payments had to complete each one as a fully separate transaction. Pay one bill, return to the bill payment home screen, start again from scratch. Repeat for every payee.

This friction came up repeatedly in client conversations, unprompted, across different clients, over an extended period. No batch workflow existed. No way to queue multiple payments and execute them together. Each additional bill meant restarting the entire process.

It was a gap that was easy to miss from an engineering perspective and impossible to miss from the front line.

---

## The Approach

I decided to do something with what I was hearing rather than log it as a complaint and move on.

Working within the constraints of a retail branch environment, limited software access due to TD's compliance requirements, I used Canva during downtime to build a UI mockup of a proposed solution. The concept was straightforward: a single bill payment screen with the ability to add multiple payees in one instance. Each row would capture a payee and a payment amount. Instead of a "Pay Now" button that executed a single transaction, the interface would allow the client to keep adding bills until they were done, then execute all of them together in one action — provided sufficient funds were available across the total.

The mockup wasn't uploaded to the submission (the portal didn't support attachments), but it existed as a backup artifact, something I could share if anyone wanted to follow up or needed the concept clarified visually.

I then wrote a structured submission for TD's internal ideation portal, a platform accessible to all retail banking staff across the organization. The document included:

- A problem statement grounded in observed client friction
- A current-state description of the broken workflow
- A future-state proposal outlining the multi-payment experience
- A brief note on how a technical team might approach implementation

The submission was a few hundred words. I wrote it to be as clear and professional as the platform itself — knowing it could be read by anyone from a branch manager to a product team.

---

## The Outcome

The submission was picked up and merged with a number of similar ideas submitted by other associates across the bank a signal that the friction I was observing wasn't isolated to my branch. Shortly after submission, the portal flagged the idea as having been moved toward implementation.

The confirmation that mattered most came from a client. Someone who had originally described the frustration to me returned to the branch and told me directly: whatever you did, it worked. The multi-payment functionality had been added to the EasyWeb platform.

The final implementation wasn't a direct reproduction of my mockup, it never is, and I didn't expect it to be. But the structured framing of the problem and the clarity of the proposed solution appeared to be enough to move the idea forward without further advocacy on my part.

This project reflects the core of how I work: I observe patterns at the point where clients meet systems, translate those patterns into structured problem statements, and find the path that gets the idea in front of the people who can act on it.
